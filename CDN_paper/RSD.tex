\begin{abstract}
%To bridge the support of scalability (fast decision in high concurrent request) and robust high cache hits performance across distinguished workload, we introduce a feature-expressive middle layer between cache eviction decision and model inference. It updates intermittently from the model but serves for each eviction decision, thus scalable and deployable. For robustness, it is designed as the combination of weighted features selected meticulously to generalize the expression of various workload. We use the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) to learn the optimal weights within current workload, which can learn the second-order interactions between features and converge rapidly.
Caches are critical for reducing request latency and origin traffic in large-scale systems. To fully leverage their limited capacity, the effectiveness of a caching system heavily relies on its eviction policy to minimize the miss ratio. However, designing eviction policies for high-concurrency production environments faces a fundamental latency-accuracy dilemma: heuristics and lightweight learning models execute efficiently but lack the expressive power to adapt to complex, shifting workloads, whereas highly capable models achieve high hit ratios but incur prohibitive inference latency.

To bridge scalability under high-concurrency requests and robust cache-hit performance across diverse workloads, we introduce a \emph{feature-expressive middle layer} between cache eviction decision and model inference to guide every real-time eviction decision. To ensure low decision latency, the middle layer is computationally lightweight to execute, while heavy model inference is decoupled to update the layer intermittently rather than synchronously blocking requests. For robustness across heterogeneous workload patterns, the middle layer is meticulously designed as a parameterized formulation coupling representative features and their second-order interactions. This intrinsically captures diverse multidimensional workload dynamics, while its compact parameter space remains easy to learn, enabling a model-agnostic architecture. Consequently, we utilize diverse background models, including highly complex Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and lightweight statistical methods such as the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), to reliably discover the optimal weights from macroscopic workload trends.

Extensive evaluations across 4,855 diverse real-world traces demonstrate that our design robustly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in miss ratio, while maintaining throughput comparable to the heuristics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{L}{ocated} near users or applications, caches serve a great proportion of requests by exploiting the pervasive skewed distribution of content, despite their limited capacity.
Caches have been deployed widely in network and computer systems, significantly improving user experience and lessening operating expenditures in today's large-scale, geographically distributed cloud services.
For example, content delivery networks (CDNs), which are distributed systems close to users, significantly reduce network delays and maintain user retention compared to traditional single-origin server models \cite{sreepriyankauppuContent2025}.

The effectiveness of a caching system heavily relies on its eviction algorithm to minimize the miss ratio.
Traditional heuristic-based algorithms \cite{oneilLRUK1993,dongheeleeLRFU2001,jiangLIRS2002,megiddoARC2003,einzigerTinyLFU2017,zhangSIEVE2024,yangs3FIFO2023} are lightweight and fast, but they are highly environment-specific and often fail when workload patterns shift. For example, LRU strictly evicts the least recently used object, which degrades performance when recent objects are not reused. LFU prioritizes frequently requested content, rendering it susceptible to cache pollution when once-popular objects lapse into long periods of inactivity.
To address these adaptability issues, learning-based schemes have been proposed, but they introduce a harsh tradeoff between model capability and inference latency.
Complex model-based approaches \cite{liuImitation2020, huRaven2022, wangLRUBase2024} offer strong fitting capabilities but suffer from high computational overhead and slow inference. For example, Raven's density estimation network and Parrot's transformers require substantial training time and incur unacceptable inference delays. Furthermore, their reliance on offline training makes them vulnerable to out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts.
On the other hand, lightweight learning models \cite{songHALP2023, zhou3Lcache2025, vietrLeCaR2018, rodriguezCACHEUS2021} achieve in-time inference by sacrificing expressive power. Algorithms like HALP (using MLPs for pairwise comparison) and 3LCACHE (using decision trees to predict reuse distance) possess weak fitting abilities, which ultimately compromises the accuracy of their eviction targets.

Realistic production cache environments demand scalable cache algorithms that deliver robust hit ratios while sustaining highly concurrent requests.
First, production caches tend to face extremely high concurrency \cite{yangLargeScaleTwitter2021,songHALP2023}. If an eviction decision takes longer than the request arrival interval, the resulting queuing delay negates the latency benefits of caching altogether. Second, production workloads are highly diverse and complicated \cite{yangs3FIFO2023}; neither static heuristics nor simple machine learning models can consistently adapt to such dynamics.
This presents a critical dilemma: Can we leverage the high accuracy of complex, slow models while maintaining the ultra-low latency required for real-time eviction? Our insight stems from identifying the bottleneck in existing learning-based caches: the tight, synchronous coupling between model inference and eviction decisions. Because the model directly dictates the eviction victim, decision latency is strictly bounded by inference time.
To address this bottleneck, we introduce a \emph{middle layer} that decouples cache eviction decisions from model inference. In this architecture, a complex background model periodically updates the middle layer, while the middle layer asynchronously guides real-time cache eviction decisions.

The design of the middle layer is the most critical. Serving as the bridge between the background model's inference and the real-time cache decision, it must satisfy three essential requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Simplicity: It must incur minimal computational overhead to ensure ultra-fast, real-time eviction decisions while remaining easily optimizable by background learning models.
    \item Universality: It must be expressive enough to capture and represent a wide variety of trace patterns and workload dynamics.
    \item Stability: It must remain robust and effective over a temporal window, resisting the noise and randomness of transient requests.
\end{itemize}

To meet these criteria, we formalize the middle layer as a parametrized scoring formula driven by a concise set of representative features.
First, the formula relies exclusively on basic multiplications and additions, guaranteeing fast execution (Simplicity).
Second,
by assigning adjustable parameters (weights) to distinct features, the formula acts as a flexible combination that can express a vast spectrum of workload preferences (Universality).
Third, rather than reacting to the stochasticity of individual requests, the formula captures the macroscopic patterns of the current workload, allowing it to maintain high performance over extended temporal windows (Stability).

Building around this middle layer, we design two complementary components: the decision layer and the model layer.
The decision layer must be equally lightweight to preserve the low-latency advantage of the scoring formula.
To achieve this, we introduce an efficient candidate selection mechanism, eliminating the need to score all cached objects during an eviction.
Meanwhile, the model layer operates asynchronously. Benefiting from the middle layer's robust designesig, our architecture is remarkably model-agnostic.
To demonstrate this flexibility, we implement and evaluate the model layer using both a lightweight statistical model (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy, CMA-ES) and a highly complex learning model (Deep Reinforcement Learning, DRL).

In this paper, we propose a novel, scalable cache eviction framework that successfully decouples heavy model inference from real-time decision-making.
Driven by a tripartite architecture, the asynchronous model layer periodically learns current workload dynamics to update the middle layer's feature weights.
Synchronously, the decision layer leverages this parameterized formula to rapidly evaluate a subset of candidates and execute cache replacements.
This framework gracefully bridges the gap between the accuracy of complex machine learning and the microsecond-level constraints of production systems.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{A Novel Decoupled Caching Paradigm:} We propose a scalable framework that resolves the fundamental conflict between model capability and decision latency. By decoupling slow model inference from the real-time execution path, our architecture preserves the high learning accuracy while sustaining the low latency required by highly concurrent requests.
    \item \textbf{A Universal and Model-Agnostic Bridge:} We design a lightweight, parameterized scoring formula combined with an efficient candidate selection process. This design is highly expressive for modeling diverse workloads while remaining hardware-friendly. Furthermore, we demonstrate its model-agnostic nature by seamlessly integrating both CMA-ES and DRL without modifying the critical data path.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Empirical Validation:} We extensively evaluate our framework on a testbed using 4,855 real-world traces. The results demonstrate that our approach consistently achieves state-of-the-art cache miss ratios, while maintaining execution speeds and CPU overheads strictly comparable to static heuristic algorithms.
\end{itemize}

Section \ref{sec: scheme} details our proposed scheme. Section \ref{sec: performance} presents the experimental evaluation, and Section \ref{sec: conclusion} concludes the paper.

The fundamental goal of our decoupled architecture is to resolve the inherent conflict between model capability and decision latency in modern caching systems. In this section, we motivate our design by examining the stringent latency constraints of highly concurrent production environments and the multidimensional complexity of real-world workloads.

\subsection{The Latency-Accuracy Dilemma in High-Concurrency Caches}

Realistic production cache environments demand algorithms that deliver robust hit ratios while sustaining highly concurrent requests \cite{songHALP2023,yangs3FIFO2023}. In modern hyperscale infrastructures, production cache traces and deployments report very high request rates, often at million-QPS scale, under strict latency objectives \cite{yangLargeScaleTwitter2021}.

Under such immense throughput, the latency of the eviction decision becomes a critical bottleneck. If a complex learning-based algorithm takes longer to infer an eviction candidate than the inter-arrival time of requests, the system transitions into a congested state. We can formulate the expected content retrieval latency $E[T]$ using basic queuing theory. Let $\lambda$ be the request arrival rate, $h$ be the cache hit ratio, $T_{backend}$ be the backend fetch penalty, and $T_{dec}$ be the inference/decision latency of the eviction algorithm. The total retrieval latency is heavily influenced by the queuing delay $T_{queue}$:

\begin{equation}
E[T] = T_{queue} + T_{dec} + (1 - h) \times T_{backend}
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}

As $T_{dec}$ increases (due to heavy model inference), the service rate $\mu = 1 / T_{dec}$ drops. When the arrival rate $\lambda$ approaches $\mu$, the queuing delay $T_{queue}$ grows exponentially, offsetting the latency reduction advantage gained by any marginal improvement in the hit ratio $h$. This creates a critical dilemma: we desire the high hit ratios provided by complex machine learning models, but their synchronous inference latency is mathematically prohibitive under high concurrency. This necessitates a paradigm shift to decouple heavy model updates from the critical path of real-time eviction.

\subsection{The Heterogeneity and Non-stationarity of Production Workloads}

Beyond concurrency challenges, the workload faced by production cache systems is notoriously diverse and complicated. Static heuristic algorithms or simple machine learning models, which implicitly assume specific, stable traffic patterns, consistently fail to adapt to such dynamics. To understand why a universal and highly expressive eviction approach is required, we contrast real production traces (e.g., 1063, wiki\_2019t, meta\_reag) with synthetic benchmarks (e.g., lrutest, lfutest, sizetest) across four critical dimensions.

\textbf{The Complexity of Reuse and Temporal Locality:}
While traditional algorithms like LRU excel when temporal locality is strictly defined by short-term reuse, production traces defy such simplicity (Fig.~\ref{fig:reuse}). In our virtual time CDF analysis, a synthetic pure-recency trace (lrutest) exhibits a nearly vertical line, indicating highly predictable and immediate re-accesses. Conversely, production traces present sub-linear growth curves spanning multiple orders of magnitude, yet their specific behaviors diverge significantly from one another. The 1063 trace displays a long-tail distribution punctuated by bursty step-increases, reflecting periodic batch accesses. In contrast, the wiki\_2019t trace exhibits an extremely smooth and steady accumulation across its tail. Meanwhile, the meta\_reag trace shows a sharp initial vertical rise denoting intense immediate reuse, which abruptly transitions into an extended, deeply polarized long-tail. This visual disparity proves that real workloads contain a complex mixture of short-term bursts and extreme long-tail periodic returns, rendering pure recency metrics fundamentally insufficient.

\textbf{The Cross-Magnitude Variance of Object Sizes:}
Size-aware caching is crucial, yet synthetic models (sizetest) often simplify object sizes into discrete, rigid tiers, appearing as stark step functions in CDF plots (Fig.~\ref{fig:size}). \section{Related Work}\label{sec: related work}
The design of cache eviction policies has been a long-standing research topic, with a rich history of both heuristic and learning-based approaches.

\textbf{Heuristic Policies.} Heuristics rely on fixed rules to evict objects. Traditional heuristic methods depend on a single observable feature. For example, LRU, LFU, Size, and FIFO policies prioritize eviction based on recency, frequency, size, and arrival time, respectively. Building on this, various composite heuristics attempt to improve performance by combining multiple features. LRU-K \cite{oneilLRUK1993} uses an LRU queue strictly for objects with $K$ or more accesses. 2Q \cite{johnson2Q1994} adds a FIFO queue before the main LRU queue to filter out one-time accesses. GDSF \cite{cherkasovaGDSF1998a} combines aged frequency and size into a single scoring mechanism. LRFU \cite{dongheeleeLRFU2001} uniquely unifies LRU and LFU by exponentially decaying historical access frequencies. ARC \cite{megiddoARC2003} dynamically balances between recency and frequency by adjusting the relative capacity of its LRU and LFU queues. wTinyLFU \cite{einzigerTinyLFU2017} adopts a space-efficient frequency sketch to actively filter out one-time accesses before they even enter the main cache space. Furthermore, sophisticated heuristics define derived metrics to capture complex workload patterns. LIRS \cite{jiangLIRS2002} distinguishes cold and hot objects via inter-access intervals. Hyperbolic \cite{blanksteinHyperbolic2017} computes a priority score by dividing the access frequency by the elapsed time since the first access. LHD \cite{beckmannLHD2018} defines the expected cache hit count per unit size as the hit density metric. Recently, hardware-efficient logic has regained popularity through methods like SIEVE \cite{zhangSIEVE2024} and S3-FIFO \cite{yangs3FIFO2023}, which deploy quick demotion and lazy promotion rules on simple FIFO structures. In addition, Belady \cite{beladyStudy1966} and BeladySize are widely used as offline-optimal references: Belady evicts the object whose next request is farthest in the future, while BeladySize evicts the object with the largest value of future reuse distance multiplied by object size. Overall, heuristics are lightweight and fast on cache misses, but fixed rule assumptions can limit robustness when workload patterns change.

\textbf{Learning-based Policies.} Learning-based schemes have been proposed to improve adaptability beyond fixed heuristics, but placing model inference directly on the miss path introduces a practical tradeoff between prediction strength and decision latency. One branch uses high-capacity models. Raven \cite{huRaven2022} constructs a density estimation network built on Gaussian mixture models to estimate the reuse distance distribution of individual objects. Parrot \cite{liuImitation2020} adapts transformers and BiDAF-based components to imitate Belady's optimal algorithm. Deep reinforcement learning has also been explored, such as LRU-BaSE \cite{wangLRUBase2024}, which applies a DRL agent to choose eviction objects from the tail portion of an LRU list. These methods can model richer patterns, but usually with higher computational overhead and longer per-miss inference time.

Another branch emphasizes lightweight predictors for online deployment. LRB \cite{songLRB2020} trains Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM) to predict reuse distance online and evicts the object with the largest predicted reuse distance. 3LCACHE \cite{zhou3Lcache2025} extends LRB by reducing online training and inference cost through adaptive sampling. HALP \cite{songHALP2023} combines heuristics and ML by using an MLP to compare only four objects at the tail of an active LRU queue. GL-Cache \cite{yangGLcache2023} clusters objects by recent access time and uses GBM models to train and predict utilization at the group level rather than per-object. In addition, LHR \cite{yanLHR2021} defines a Hazard Rate metric that is the probability an object will be requested again within a time window, and evicts the object with the lowest hazard score. Instead of directly learning object scores, LeCaR \cite{vietrLeCaR2018} and CACHEUS \cite{rodriguezCACHEUS2021} use online learning with regret minimization to control the dynamic use of two heuristic cache replacement policies.

Related but different from the above online learning designs, some studies imitate Belady-style decisions through offline analysis of historical traces. Hawkeye \cite{jainHawkeye2016} uses retrospective Belady simulation on past access history to build occupancy-based labels of cache-friendly versus cache-unfriendly objects. LFO \cite{bergerLFO2018} approximates near-OPT decisions by formulating best-case caching as a min-cost flow problem over historical snapshots. These methods avoid per-miss model inference on the critical path, but their dependence on historical assumptions makes them sensitive to out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts and less suitable as plug-and-play policies across changing deployment environments.

Taken together, prior work still leaves an unresolved tension among predictive capability, real-time decision latency, and adaptation to changing workloads. This tension motivates the decoupled architecture proposed in this paper.

\section{Design of RSD}\label{sec: scheme}
\subsection{Overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates the proposed framework, which is organized into three loosely coupled layers: a \emph{decision layer} on the critical path of every eviction, a \emph{middle layer} that exposes a lightweight parametric scoring rule, and a \emph{model layer} that periodically updates the parameters of the middle layer according to the current workload.

The three layers have distinct responsibilities. The decision layer first selects a compact candidate pool, then computes the required features for only those candidates, sends the features to the middle layer for scoring, and evicts the lowest-scoring object. The middle layer is the common interface between real-time decision making and background learning: it converts object features into scalar eviction priorities through a small number of interpretable weights. The model layer observes epoch-level cache statistics, searches for better weights, and periodically refreshes the middle-layer parameters. Because the data path depends only on the scoring interface rather than on the internal learner, the framework is model-agnostic by construction.

The key design principle is decoupling. Existing learning-based cache policies often bind model inference to each individual eviction, which makes decision latency directly proportional to model complexity. In contrast, our framework allows the model layer to run asynchronously and intermittently, while the decision layer always executes a fixed and hardware-friendly scoring procedure. This decoupling enables the use of sophisticated background learners without sacrificing the microsecond-level responsiveness required by highly concurrent cache systems. More importantly, the proposed decomposition changes the learning target itself. Instead of asking the model to directly identify the eviction victim for every miss, we ask it to learn a workload-level \emph{preference profile} over a small set of interpretable features. This shift from per-object prediction to workload-level preference learning simultaneously reduces online complexity and improves cross-workload adaptability.

\subsection{Middle Layer Design}
The middle layer is the central abstraction of our framework. It must simultaneously satisfy the three requirements identified in Section~\ref{sec: motivation}: simplicity, universality, and stability. Simplicity requires the score to be inexpensive enough for per-eviction execution. Universality requires it to represent heterogeneous workloads that exhibit different age, frequency, and size preferences. Stability requires the score to depend on workload-level object properties rather than transient identifiers or instantaneous queue positions.

These requirements naturally lead to a feature-based parametric score. We select three primary object descriptors that are universally available in practical caches and directly related to eviction quality: age, frequency, and size. Age captures temporal locality, frequency reflects long-term popularity, and size models the opportunity cost of occupying cache space. However, a plain linear combination of these basic features is insufficient for production workloads, because real traces are dominated by cross-dimensional interactions. For example, a large object with moderate frequency may still be less valuable than a small object with the same frequency, and an old object with high historical frequency may remain beneficial if its popularity-to-staleness ratio is strong.

To preserve efficiency while increasing expressiveness, we augment the three primary features with pairwise interaction terms instead of replacing the middle layer with a neural predictor. This choice is deliberate. A fully learned per-object predictor would introduce a much larger parameter space, slower convergence, and higher synchronization cost between the background learner and the data path. In contrast, pairwise interactions retain interpretability, keep the parameter dimension small, and still expose second-order coupling information to the model layer.

Under the default implementation, raw feature values are first transformed by a logarithmic compressor, namely
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}=\log(1+x),
\end{equation}
which suppresses the extreme scale disparity across age, access count, and object size. Let $a$, $f$, and $s$ denote the transformed age, frequency, and size of a candidate object, respectively. The default middle-layer score is
\begin{equation}
S(o) = -w_1 a + w_2 f - w_3 s + w_4 \frac{f}{a} + w_5 \frac{f}{s} - w_6 as,
\label{eq:middle-score}
\end{equation}
where each weight $w_i \in [0,1]$ is periodically updated by the model layer. The sign convention is fixed by cache semantics: large age and large size make an object more evictable, whereas large frequency makes it more retainable. This sign-decoupled parameterization is practically important because it constrains the learner to search only over the magnitude of each factor rather than relearning obvious polarity.

Equation~\ref{eq:middle-score} is expressive enough to cover a broad family of classical heuristics as special cases or soft variants. For instance, emphasizing only $a$ approximates age-based eviction, emphasizing $f$ approximates frequency-oriented retention, and emphasizing $s$ favors size-aware replacement. More importantly, the interaction terms allow the score to capture multidimensional tradeoffs that static heuristics cannot represent. The term $f/a$ retains objects that are both popular and recently useful, $f/s$ prefers compact objects with high value density, and $as$ suppresses old large objects that are expensive to keep.

Not every interaction term is equally informative for every workload. Therefore, the design further includes an adaptive pruning mechanism for compound terms. During warm-up, the cache scans resident objects and estimates Pearson correlations between a compound feature and its constituent base features. If a compound term is almost fully explained by a base feature, it is treated as redundant and can be disabled. In particular, the $f/a$ term is conservatively removed in the adaptive variant because it is often strongly collinear with frequency in real traces. The $f/s$ term is disabled when $\mathrm{Pearson}(f, f/s)$ is close to one, which typically occurs when object sizes show very limited variation. Similarly, the $as$ term can be removed when $\mathrm{Pearson}(a, as)$ indicates that multiplying by size adds little independent information. This mechanism preserves universality without forcing unnecessary dimensions on structurally simpler workloads.

The resulting middle layer provides a favorable balance between expressiveness and deployability. It is substantially richer than a single-feature heuristic, yet still compact enough to be optimized by either statistical search or reinforcement learning while keeping the critical path lightweight. In effect, it acts as an information bottleneck with carefully preserved semantics: rich enough to encode workload preference, but small enough to be updated reliably and executed cheaply.

\subsection{Decision Layer Design}
The decision layer executes on every cache miss that requires replacement. Its objective is to preserve eviction quality under the middle-layer score while avoiding full-cache scanning. This is achieved through a three-step pipeline: candidate selection, feature computation, and batched eviction.

\textbf{1) Candidate selection.} In realistic deployments, a cache
can contain tens of thousands to millions of objects. Even
a simple scoring function becomes expensive if evaluated
over all residents on every eviction. We therefore construct a compact candidate pool consisting of objects that are highly likely to yield the lowest eviction score.

To efficiently assemble this pool, we actively track objects that exhibit extreme values across the three fundamental dimensions: age, frequency, and size. The supporting structures are designed according to each feature's value range and update dynamics, so that both extraction and maintenance remain lightweight in the online path. For age, we maintain an age-ordered queue and sample objects directly from its tail to obtain the stalest residents. For frequency, we maintain count-indexed linked lists and sample from the lowest-count lists. For size, objects are grouped into discrete size-range buckets, where each bucket represents an interval of sizes rather than one exact size, and candidates are sampled from the buckets covering the largest ranges. The maintenance overhead of these structures is low: each request triggers only local object insertion or movement in the corresponding queue, list, or bucket, without any global reorder or full-cache traversal. Candidates systematically drawn from these structures are called \emph{feature-extreme candidates}, as they represent the worst objects along at least one individual dimension.

The quota for feature-extreme candidates is not fixed across candidate sources, namely, the age-based, frequency-based, and size-based sources. Instead, the decision layer adaptively reallocates quota according to how often each source produces the final eviction object. Let $n_i^{\mathrm{evict}}$ denote the number of evictions which came from source $i$ in the last period. The next-period quota for source $i$ is adjusted in proportion to its observed contribution:
\begin{equation}
n_i^{\mathrm{cand}} \propto n_i^{\mathrm{evict}},
\label{eq:adaptive-budget}
\end{equation}
Meanwhile, it is subject to a minimum quota to prevent starvation. This adaptive mechanism allows the decision layer to spend more search effort on the candidate sources that are currently more predictive, while still preserving diversity in candidate generation.

Feature-extreme candidates alone are insufficient because the truly optimal eviction target may not be strictly extreme in any single feature. Real caches often contain objects that are only moderately poor along each individual dimension, yet become the worst target under joint scoring. To cover this case, we also sample a set of \emph{random candidates} from current cached objects. This random set complements the feature-extreme pool and improves exploration over the full candidate space.

\textbf{2) Feature computation.} After candidate generation, the decision layer computes only the required features for each candidate. The raw age of an object is measured by the request distance since its last access, frequency is its access count, and size is its occupied bytes. Because these quantities naturally differ by several orders of magnitude, direct combination would cause numerical overflow and shadow smaller features. To address this, the decision layer first applies a logarithmic transformation to compress the dynamic range of each metric. It then divides each transformed feature by a tracked running maximum to normalize the scales into a standardized interval. This  ensures stability during dynamic score aggregation.

\textbf{3) Batched eviction execution.} The decision layer further amortizes scoring overhead through a small batch-eviction queue. After one scoring round, the worst candidates are temporarily buffered and can be directly consumed by subsequent evictions as long as they serve the same cache miss. To prevent delayed evictions from degrading the hit ratio, this queue is strictly invalidated upon the arrival of any new request. This optimization effectively eliminates repeated candidate sampling and score computation when inserting a single large object mandates evicting multiple smaller ones, preventing latency spikes during size-induced batched evictions.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:decision-layer} summarizes the online eviction procedure. When a miss occurs and the cache space is insufficient for the incoming object, the decision layer eviction procedure is invoked. If the batch queue is empty, a new candidate pool $P$ is assembled according to the adaptively allocated feature-extreme quota and the random samples. Normalized features are computed for each caching metric $j \in \{a,f,s\}$ and used to score each candidate. The object $O$ with the minimum score is evicted, and remaining low-score candidates are buffered to serve further space requirements within the same request cycle.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
	\caption{Decision-layer eviction procedure.}
	\label{alg:decision-layer}
	\KwIn{Missed request $R$, current cache state}
	\KwOut{Evicted object(s)}
	\SetAlgoLined
	clear the batch-eviction queue\;
	\If{at start of a new epoch}{
		update quota $n_i^{\mathrm{cand}}$ for each source $i$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-budget}\;
	}
	\While{free space $<$ size($R$)}{
		\eIf{batch eviction queue is not empty}{
			$O \gets$ pop candidate from the queue\;
		}{
			$P \gets \emptyset$\;
			add $n_i^{\mathrm{cand}}$ candidates to $P$ from each source $i$\;
			add random candidates to $P$\;
			\ForEach{$c \in P$}{
				\ForEach{feature $j \in \{a, f, s\}$}{
					$\tilde{f}_j(c) \gets \frac{\log(1 + f_j(c))}{\max(\log(1+f_j))}$\;
				}
				compute score $S(c)$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:middle-score} using $\tilde{f}_j(c)$\;
			}
			$O \gets \arg\min_{c \in P} S(c)$\;
			buffer remaining low-score candidates from $P$ into queue\;
		}
		evict $O$ and update statistics for source $i$ of $O$\;
	}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Model Layer Design}
% [OLD-Intro] The model layer periodically updates the middle-layer weights
% using epoch-level statistics, and can run asynchronously at a much lower
% frequency than cache accesses.
% [OLD-Intro] The framework is model-agnostic: any learner that maps aggregate
% feedback to a small weight vector can be plugged in (CMA-ES or DRL).

The model layer governs the adaptation of the caching system by periodically updating the middle-layer weight vector $\mathbf{w}\in[0,1]^d$. Unlike traditional learning-based caches that couple model inference with real-time eviction, our design delegates heavy learning to an asynchronous background process operating on an epoch scale. 

This architectural decoupling yields significant advantages in both system scalability and algorithmic robustness. First, it removes the latency bottleneck. By taking the model off the critical data path, the system effortlessly sustains ultra-high concurrency. Decisions are made in microseconds, while the model is updated at much larger timescales. Second, epoch-scale learning acts as a natural low-pass filter. Instead of being misled by the stochastic noise and transient spikes of individual requests, the model layer synthesizes macro-patterns from aggregated feedback such as epoch-level miss ratios. Third, it gracefully sidesteps the curse of dimensionality. By compressing the combinatorial search over millions of objects into a $d$-dimensional continuous policy space, the framework transforms an intractable selection problem into a tractable continuous optimization problem that is highly amenable to rapid learning. Ultimately, through these three architectural innovations, the model layer is elevated from a mere eviction predictor to a \emph{policy-shaping module}. It determines the underlying preference structure of the cache, leaving the fast decision layer to simply execute the formulated policy.

Guided by this philosophy, the model layer no longer focuses on pinpointing specific victims. Instead, it continuously executes a stable tripartite protocol: evaluating the current preference vector over an entire epoch, distilling a refined vector from the aggregated feedback, and updating the middle layer with the new policy. 

\textbf{Unified learning objective.}
Let epoch $t$ denote an evaluation window containing a sequence of requests.
Model-layer learning aims to minimize the epoch-level miss ratio,
which yields a low-dimensional black-box optimization over the middle-layer
policy space:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}_t^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\in[0,1]^d} \mathrm{MR}_t(\mathbf{w}).
\label{eq:model-argmin}
\end{equation}

This formulation is model-agnostic: any optimizer that maps
epoch summaries to improved $\mathbf{w}$ can be plugged in. We instantiate two
algorithmic families, DRL and CMA-ES, to demonstrate this property.

\textbf{1) DRL-based model layer.}
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) is a powerful machine learning paradigm where an intelligent agent trains a deep neural network to maximize cumulative future rewards through trial-and-error interactions. In our framework, the DRL agent operates as an epoch-level policy optimizer. At the start of each epoch, it observes the summarized state of the cache environment, predicts the optimal middle-layer weights, and later receives an aggregated reward based on the caching performance during that epoch. Through continuous repetitions, it systematically learns a policy mapping from cache states to optimal weights.

This formulation aligns seamlessly with the episodic nature of our architecture and unlocks the true potential of DRL in real-time caching. First, it circumvents the notorious \emph{inference wall}. By pushing computationally heavy neural network inference to the epoch boundary, it prevents DRL from throttling microsecond-level cache operations. Second, it elegantly mitigates the \emph{delayed credit assignment} problem. By treating an entire epoch as a single transition step in a Markov Decision Process (MDP), the DRL agent receives a robust, aggregated reward signal. This filters out the chaotic volatility of individual requests, enabling the neural network to focus entirely on learning enduring workload trends and optimizing long-term cache performance.

At epoch boundary $t$, the agent reads a comprehensive summary state $s_t$ that characterizes the current caching dynamics. Specifically, $s_t$ encompasses the recent epoch miss ratio alongside the aggregated distribution features of hits and misses, namely, the mean and variance of fundamental object properties (such as recency, frequency, and size) computed separately for objects that hit and missed during the epoch. Based on $s_t$, the neural network directly outputs an action corresponding to the continuous weight vector $\mathbf{w}_t \in [0,1]^d$. After executing the cache eviction policy directed by $\mathbf{w}_t$ for an entire epoch, the agent receives a reward
\begin{equation}
r_t = -\mathrm{MR}_t(\mathbf{w}_t),
\label{eq:drl-reward}
\end{equation}
and transitions to a new state $s_{t+1}$. The training objective is to learn an optimal policy $\pi$ that maximizes the expected return:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r_t\right],
\label{eq:drl-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in (0,1]$ is the discount factor. Through this trajectory, DRL successfully exploits its powerful representation capacity without entering the latency-critical replacement loop.

\textbf{2) CMA-ES-based model layer.}
While DRL showcases the architectural capability to support heavy neural networks, the inherent expressiveness of our middle-layer design means that even simpler models can achieve exceptional performance. To demonstrate this, we also instantiate the model layer using a purely statistical, lightweight alternative: the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES). CMA-ES is a widely used derivative-free optimization algorithm for continuous spaces. It updates weights by repeated trial and selection.
At each generation, it samples several weight vectors, evaluates their miss
ratios, keeps the better ones, and shifts the next sampling distribution toward
those better results.

This method seamlessly fits our objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:model-argmin}.
Because the real cache environment is continuous but highly non-convex, full of
local optima, and lacks reliable analytical gradients, direct mathematical
optimization fails. CMA-ES thrives in this exact regime.
By intrinsically adapting a covariance matrix, it naturally decodes the complex,
second-order geometrical dependencies among feature dimensions, such as the
correlation between frequency and recency. By retaining the most successful
candidates, it ensures steady navigation through shifting workloads.


In each generation, candidate weights are sampled as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_t,\sigma_t^2\mathbf{C}_t),
\label{eq:cma-sample}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{m}_t$ is the current center of candidate weights, $\sigma_t$ controls the overall search step, and $\mathbf{C}_t$ controls how different dimensions move together.
Each candidate $\mathbf{w}_k$ is evaluated by one epoch rollout to obtain
$\mathrm{MR}_t(\mathbf{w}_k)$.
The optimizer then updates $\mathbf{m}_{t+1}$ toward better candidates, and
adjusts $\mathbf{C}_{t+1}$ and $\sigma_{t+1}$ based on recent improvements.

Specifically, we employ the Active CMA-ES with Increasing Population Size (aIPOP) variant\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CMA-ES/libcmaes}}. Building upon standard CMA-ES, aIPOP actively accelerates convergence by making negative updates to the covariance matrix for unsuccessful directions. Moreover, when the search converges to a local optimum, it triggers a restart with an increasing population size. This systematic expansion of the search space effectively climbs out of deep local minima, making it exceptionally robust to the highly multimodal and shifting nature of real-world cache environments.

Based on the interchangeable role of the optimization models, Algorithm~\ref{alg:model-layer} summarizes the generalized framework interface. Rather than coupling specific logic inside the eviction path, the system encapsulates any optimizer into an abstract routine that observes epoch summaries, steps an internal solver, and emits refined parameters.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Generalized model-layer process.}
\label{alg:model-layer}
\KwIn{Abstract optimizer context $\Theta$, summary state $s$, and epoch feedback $\mathcal{D}$}
\KwOut{Refreshed middle-layer weights $\mathbf{w}$}
\SetAlgoLined
parse the epoch summary $s_t$ and evaluate cache miss ratio $\mathrm{MR}_t$\;
pass the aggregated feedback $\mathcal{D}_t$ to the unified optimization oracle\;
execute one step of parameter adjustment internally (e.g., gradient ascent for DRL or covariance update for CMA-ES)\;
generate the next optimal weight vector $\mathbf{w}_{t+1} \in [0,1]^d$ from the internal model\;
propagate the updated weights $\mathbf{w}_{t+1}$ to the fast middle-layer for subsequent evictions\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Scalability}
Scalability is evaluated by throughput under high-pressure trace replay.
Instead of adding a heavy predictor on every miss, RSD executes only the lightweight middle-layer score in the latency-critical path and performs model optimization asynchronously.
This design keeps per-decision computation close to heuristic-level cost while preserving adaptability.
In our evaluation, RSD sustains high throughput with modest overhead growth as workload intensity increases, validating that the decoupled architecture is deployable for high-concurrency environments.
